    Q&A prep · one page

    Everything to know in ~10 minutes

    Core story, numbers, slide-by-slide lines, and likely questions with short answers. Chapter links are only for when you need more.

    ▶ SlidesVideoSources: Llecture BFahim Ch. 8

    1Core story (say this if you blank)

    1. What: FCC cracks heavy gas oil (VGO, 316–566 °C) into gasoline, LPG olefins and cycle oils on an acidic zeolite catalyst. Bpp. 199–200
    2. How: hot regenerated catalyst + feed rise through a riser (480–550 °C, 1–5 bar, a few seconds); cyclones separate; steam strips the catalyst. Bpp. 208, 210
    3. Regenerate: air burns the coke off; the reheated catalyst returns and carries the heat for the endothermic cracking. Lp. 17
    4. Chemistry: carbenium-ion chain on acid sites: protonation → β-scission → hydride transfer. Lpp. 11–12
    5. Why it matters: main gasoline maker ("heart of the refinery") + C₃/C₄ olefins for alkylation. Bp. 199

    4Likely questions (answer aloud, then open)

    Process

    Why is it called "fluid" catalytic cracking?
    The ~70 µm catalyst powder behaves like a fluid when aerated, so it can circulate continuously between reactor and regenerator. Lp. 16
    Where does the heat for cracking come from?
    Burning coke in the regenerator. Hot catalyst carries it to the riser. At steady state, heat made in the regenerator = heat used in the reactor, so coke yield is set by the heat balance. Bpp. 199, 211
    Why a riser and not a dense fluidised bed?
    Zeolites are ~100× more active; a short, plug-flow contact with fast separation prevents gasoline over-cracking to gas and coke. Dense beds were used before ~1965. Lpp. 12, 16, 19
    Why low pressure and high temperature?
    Cracking makes many more moles (favoured by low P) and is strongly endothermic (needs high T). Bp. 208
    What does the stripper do?
    Steam + baffles remove oil adsorbed on spent catalyst so it is recovered as product instead of being burned. Bp. 210
    Partial vs complete combustion in the regenerator?
    Partial (to CO): older, about half the air, needs a CO boiler. Complete (to CO₂): newer, lower carbon left on catalyst (0.01–0.4 wt%) → more active, selective catalyst. Lp. 17
    Fluidisation regime in each vessel?
    Riser: pneumatic transport. Regenerator: turbulent. Stripper and standpipe: bubbling. BTable 8.5
    What happens to the flue gas?
    Cyclones → power-recovery expander → CO boiler (if CO present) → electrostatic precipitator → stack. Bp. 211 Lpp. 16–17
    Typical riser size?
    Example: 0.8 m diameter × 33 m tall, catalyst 144 kg/s, feed 20 kg/s (C/O 7.2). BEx. E8.4

    Catalyst and feed

    Why must the catalyst be acidic?
    It must donate a proton (Brønsted site) to form the carbenium ion; cracking needs the strongest acid sites. Lpp. 10, 12
    Zeolite vs amorphous silica–alumina?
    Zeolite: ~100× activity, more gasoline, less coke and dry gas, more iso-butane; octane 2–3 numbers lower. Lpp. 12–13
    What is the matrix for?
    Binder (cohesion) + kaolin filler (body) give density and attrition resistance; additives promote CO → CO₂ and trap SOₓ. Bp. 209
    What does ZSM-5 do?
    Shape-selective: only linear paraffins enter and crack to C₃/C₄, so gasoline is richer in iso-paraffins + aromatics. ~5 % gives about +1 RON. Bp. 209
    How does the catalyst deactivate?
    Coke (temporary, burned each cycle); V (activity loss) and Ni (coke + H₂); nitrogen neutralises acid sites; overheating in the regenerator. Lpp. 16, 20 Bp. 201
    How are metals handled?
    Up to 15 ppm Ni + V: antimony passivation (coke −15 %, H₂ −45 %). Above that with CCR > 3 %: hydroprocess the feed. Lp. 20
    Why hydrotreat the feed?
    Removes S, N, metals and lowers carbon residue → protects catalyst, more gasoline, less coke. Bp. 201 Lp. 20

    Chemistry

    Write β-scission.
    \(\ce{R-CH^+-CH2-CH2-R' -> R-CH=CH2 + {}^{+}CH2-R'}\): the C–C bond β to the charge breaks → olefin + smaller ion. BEq. 8.3
    Carbenium ion stability, and why it matters?
    Tertiary > secondary > primary. Ions rearrange to tertiary → branched (iso) products → high octane, lots of iso-butane. Lpp. 11–12
    What is hydrogen transfer?
    \(\ce{3 C_{n}H_{2n} + C_{m}H_{2m} -> 3 C_{n}H_{2n+2} + C_{m}H_{2m-6}}\): olefin + naphthene → paraffin + aromatic. Lowers olefins, adds coke; strong on zeolites. BEq. 8.7
    How does coke form?
    Aromatics and olefins lose hydrogen and condense/polymerise on the catalyst. BEq. 8.8
    Carbonium or carbenium?
    Same species here. Lecture says carbonium; the textbook uses carbenium, the modern name. Lp. 9 Bp. 204
    Is the overall reaction endo- or exothermic?
    Endothermic. Cracking, dealkylation and dehydrogenation absorb more than H-transfer, isomerisation and cyclisation release. Bp. 208 Lp. 10

    Products and optimisation

    Define conversion.
    (feed − cycle stock) / feed × 100 by volume = 100 − gas-oil yield; cycle stock = LCO + HCO. BEq. 8.12
    Why can liquid volume yield exceed 100 %?
    Products are less dense than the feed. Lp. 23
    How do you increase gasoline?
    Zeolite catalyst, short contact + fast separation, optimum (not maximum) riser temperature, hydrotreated feed, full CO₂ regeneration. Lpp. 12–20
    What if severity is too high?
    Gasoline over-cracks to gas and coke: gasoline yield passes through a maximum with conversion. BEx. E8.3
    How do you get more propylene?
    ZSM-5, higher severity, or DCC / Petro-FCC designs (22 vs 4.7 wt% propylene). BTable 8.8
    Uses of LCO and slurry?
    LCO: diesel after hydrotreating, or fuel diluent. Clarified slurry: fuel oil, aromatic solvent, or recycle to the riser. BTable 8.2, p. 211
    FCC vs hydrocracking?
    FCC adds no hydrogen and rejects carbon as coke; hydrocracking adds hydrogen and costs more. The lecture notes gas oils could go to a hydrocracker for diesel, but FCC is the cheaper process. Lp. 20
