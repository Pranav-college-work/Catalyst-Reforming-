    Chapter 04 · chemistry

    Reactions and reaction mechanism

    What cracks into what, which reactions give off heat and which absorb it, and the carbenium-ion chain on the acid catalyst, walked through one step at a time for n-octane.

    Sources: Lpp. 9–13, 24 B§8.5–8.6, Table 8.3Figures: 4.1–4.2

    4.1The big picture: reaction network

    • Primary cracking: VGO → gasoline + LCO (the desired step). Bp. 202
    • Secondary reactions must be limited: hydrogen transfer lowers gasoline yield, and cycloaddition can lead to coke. Bp. 202
    • Large molecules → smaller fragments that carry double bonds. The hydrogen needed for that is balanced by hydrogen transfer, cyclisation and aromatisation. Lp. 9
    • Basic reaction: C–C scission of paraffins, cycloparaffins and aromatic side chains gives olefins and smaller paraffins. A small amount of aromatics and olefins condense to coke. Lp. 24

    4.2Reactions by hydrocarbon type

    Feed moleculeReactions in the FCC
    ParaffinsCracking → paraffins + olefins
    OlefinsCracking → LPG olefins · cyclisation → naphthenes · isomerisation → branched olefins and paraffins · H-transfer → paraffins · cyclisation → coke
    NaphthenesCracking → olefins · dehydrogenation → aromatics · isomerisation → restructured naphthenes
    AromaticsSide-chain cracking → aromatics + olefins · transalkylation → alkylaromatics · dehydrogenation → polyaromatics → coke

    Source: Bp. 206

    Key equations

    \[ \ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2} -> C_{m}H_{2m+2} + C_{p}H_{2p}} \qquad (n = m + p) \]
    paraffin → paraffin + olefin

    Example: \(\ce{n-C10H22 -> n-C7H16 + C3H6}\). BTable 8.3 Lp. 2

    \[ \ce{1-C8H16 -> 2 C4H8} \]
    olefin cracking

    Olefins crack much faster than paraffins and isomerise rapidly. BTable 8.3 Lp. 11

    \[ \ce{C6H5-CH2CH3 -> C6H6 + CH2=CH2} \]
    (8.5) dealkylation

    The aromatic ring is stable and does not crack; side chains come off by β-scission. BEq. 8.5 Lp. 11

    \[ \underbrace{\ce{3 C_{n}H_{2n}}}_{\text{olefin}} + \underbrace{\ce{C_{m}H_{2m}}}_{\text{naphthene}} \ce{->} \underbrace{\ce{3 C_{n}H_{2n+2}}}_{\text{paraffin}} + \underbrace{\ce{C_{m}H_{2m-6}}}_{\text{aromatic}} \]
    (8.7) H-transfer

    Turns reactive olefins into stable paraffins and aromatics. It lowers gasoline olefins, contributes to coke, and is strong on zeolites. BEq. 8.7, p. 205

    \[ \text{aromatics, cyclo-olefins} \xrightarrow[\text{alkylation, condensation, polymerisation}]{\text{H loss}} \text{coke} \]
    (8.8) coke formation

    Hydrogen-deficient molecules lose more hydrogen and condense to coke; the hydrogen they give up saturates olefins to paraffins (Eq. 8.9). BEqs 8.8–8.9

    Reactivity order

    • Olefins crack much faster than paraffins and isomerise rapidly. Lp. 11
    • Naphthenes crack faster than the corresponding paraffins. Lp. 11
    • Paraffins: rate rises with carbon number, but so does coke formation; the cracked gas is rich in C₃ and C₄. Lp. 11
    • Aromatics: the ring survives; alkyl groups are removed easily by β-scission. Lp. 11

    4.4How the carbenium ion forms

    \[ \ce{R-CH=CH2 + H+ -> R-CH^+-CH3} \]
    protonation (Brønsted site)

    An acid site donates a proton to an olefin, giving a secondary carbenium ion. Lp. 9 BEq. 8.2

    • Lewis acids (BF₃, AlCl₃, FeCl₃) can also add to olefins and generate the ion. Lp. 10
    • A carbenium ion can live from a fraction of a second to minutes. Lp. 10
    • Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary. Ions rearrange toward the tertiary form, which is why FCC products are branched. Lp. 11
    Name check

    The lecture says carbonium ion; the textbook says carbenium ion. Both mean the positively charged, three-bonded carbon species in this mechanism; "carbenium" is the modern name. Lp. 9 Bp. 204

    4.5Mechanism step by step: cracking n-octane

    Use the tabs or Next / Back. The steps follow the lecture's five steps for n-C₈H₁₈ plus the book's termination step.

    Initiation: a little thermal cracking makes the first olefin

    \[ \ce{n-C8H18 -> CH4 + CH3(CH2)4CH=CH2} \]
    • Mild thermal cracking of the paraffin gives methane and 1-heptene, the first olefin. Lpp. 11–12 BEq. 8.1
    • The olefin is needed because it is what the acid site can protonate.

    Proton addition (proton shift) on a Brønsted site

    \[ \ce{CH3(CH2)4CH=CH2 + H+ ->[\text{zeolite}] CH3(CH2)4CH^+CH3} \]
    • The zeolite's acidic Al–O(H)–Si site gives a proton to the olefin. BEq. 8.2 Lp. 12
    • Product: a secondary carbenium ion, C₇H₁₅⁺, with the charge on C-2.

    β-scission: the C–C bond β to the charge breaks

    \[ \ce{CH3CH^+CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 -> CH3CH=CH2 + {}^{+}CH2CH2CH2CH3} \]
    • The bond between the α and β carbons (counted from C⁺) breaks. BEq. 8.3
    • Products: an olefin (propene) and a new, smaller carbenium ion (primary C₄H₉⁺). Lp. 12
    • This is the cracking step itself. Fig 4.2 below draws it.

    Rearrangement toward the more stable ion

    \[ \ce{{}^{+}CH2CH2CH2CH3 -> CH3CH^+CH2CH3 -> (CH3)3C^+} \]
    • Hydride and methyl shifts move the charge: primary → secondary → tertiary. Lp. 12
    • Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary. This is the origin of the iso-paraffins in FCC gasoline and LPG. Lp. 11 BEq. 8.10

    Hydrogen (hydride) transfer: the chain propagates

    \[ \ce{(CH3)3C^+ + n-C8H18 -> (CH3)3CH + C8H17^+} \]
    • The tertiary ion takes a hydride (H⁻) from a fresh paraffin molecule, giving isobutane. Lp. 12
    • The paraffin becomes a new carbenium ion, which goes back to β-scission: a chain reaction. Bp. 204

    Termination

    • The ion gives a proton back to the catalyst and becomes an olefin; or
    • it picks up a hydride ion from a donor (e.g. coke) and becomes a paraffin. Bp. 204
    \[ \ce{R^+ -> R'CH=CH2 + H+} \qquad \ce{R^+ + H^- -> RH} \]

    4.6Secondary reactions

    \[ \ce{R-CH2-CH2-CH^+-CH3 -> R-CH2-C^+(CH3)-CH3} \]
    (8.10) isomerisation

    Secondary → tertiary ion; net result: paraffins and olefins become iso-paraffins. BEq. 8.10

    • Cyclisation: olefins cyclise to naphthenes and possibly further to coke. BEq. 8.11, p. 206
    • Hydrogen transfer is caused by the same chemistry and drives secondary cracking of gasoline. Bp. 205
    • With zeolites: \(\ce{O + N -> P + A}\), \(\ce{O -> P + A}\), cyclo-olefins → N + A, giving fewer olefins and more paraffins and aromatics. Lp. 13
    Why this matters for product quality
    • Branched products ← ions rearrange to tertiary form → higher octane, more isobutane.
    • Aromatics ← naphthene dehydrogenation and H-transfer → higher octane.
    • C₃/C₄-rich gas ← β-scission gives olefins and ions in the C₃+ range. Lp. 11
    • Coke ← aromatics and olefins condensing → fuel for the regenerator, but too much lowers yields.

    4.7Quick check

    Write the β-scission of the 2-heptyl ion.
    \(\ce{CH3CH^+CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 -> CH3CH=CH2 + {}^{+}CH2CH2CH2CH3}\): propene plus a primary butyl ion, which then rearranges to the tertiary butyl ion. BEq. 8.3
    Why does FCC make so much isobutane?
    The butyl ion rearranges to the stable tertiary (CH₃)₃C⁺ ion, which takes a hydride from a paraffin to become isobutane (step 5). Zeolite H-transfer increases this further. Lpp. 12–13
    Is the net FCC reaction endothermic or exothermic? Why?
    Endothermic. Cracking, dealkylation and dehydrogenation absorb heat and outweigh the exothermic H-transfer, isomerisation and cyclisation. Bp. 208 Lp. 10
    What stops the chain?
    The ion loses a proton to the catalyst (becomes an olefin) or gains a hydride from a donor such as coke (becomes a paraffin). Bp. 204
