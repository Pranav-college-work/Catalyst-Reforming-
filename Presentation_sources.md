### 2. Types of catalytic cracking

Lecture: Cracking and Reforming 1, supplied course PDF, pp. 14–18. Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Hydrocracking is a separate hydrogen-addition process and is outside the scope of this deck.

### 3. Feedstock and catalyst

Lecture: Cracking and Reforming 1, supplied course PDF, pp. 12–16 and 20. Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Printed page numbers are used. PDF page = printed page + 17., sections 8.3 and 8.7. Gas oil boiling range is a feed distillation range, not reactor temperature. The lecture gives about 70 micrometres and the book about 75 micrometres for catalyst particles. Zeolite Y is the acid cracking component. Matrix, binder and kaolin contribute accessibility and particle strength. ZSM-5 is an additive used to increase light olefins and gasoline octane, with a gasoline yield trade-off. Hydrotreating reduces sulfur, nitrogen and metals. The lecture misspells a rare-earth example as cesium; that example is intentionally not reproduced. Nickel promotes hydrogen and coke formation, whereas vanadium damages zeolite activity.

### 6. Reactions in FCC

Lecture: Cracking and Reforming 1, supplied course PDF, pp. 9–13 and 24. Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Printed page numbers are used. PDF page = printed page + 17., sections 8.5 and 8.6. The equations are representative balanced examples rather than a unique overall FCC stoichiometry. Cracking of paraffins yields a smaller paraffin and olefin. Hydrogen transfer is illustrated using three propene molecules and a cyclohexane donor: three propane molecules plus benzene result. Isomerisation enriches branched hydrocarbons. Aromatic side chains can crack while aromatic rings are comparatively resistant. Condensation and hydrogen loss lead to polyaromatic coke. Regeneration equation treats coke as carbon for simplicity; real coke contains hydrogen and other elements and partial CO combustion can occur. Overall cracking is endothermic and coke combustion is exothermic.

### 7. Reaction mechanism

Lecture: Cracking and Reforming 1, supplied course PDF, pp. 11–12. Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Printed page numbers are used. PDF page = printed page + 17., equations 8.1–8.3 and 8.10, pp. 204–205. Supplement: Semih Eser, Penn State FSC 432, Ionic Chain Reactions, https://courses.ems.psu.edu/fsc432/node/721 (accessed 29 September 2026). H–Z denotes a Brønsted acid site and Z− the conjugate framework site. The equations illustrate the olefin/carbenium route: protonation, beta scission, hydride transfer and proton return. Initial olefins may come from mild thermal cracking. Direct paraffin activation and carbonium-ion paths also exist. Beta scission breaks the C–C bond between the alpha and beta carbons relative to the charged carbon, creating an olefin and a smaller carbenium ion. Rearrangements via hydride/alkyl shifts favour more stable secondary/tertiary ions and help explain branched products. Hydride transfer propagates the ionic chain, whereas deprotonation restores a Brønsted site. Carbonium and carbenium are distinguished in modern terminology; the lecture sometimes uses carbonium broadly.

### 8. Riser reactor

Lecture: Cracking and Reforming 1, supplied course PDF, pp. 13 and 16. Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Printed page numbers are used. PDF page = printed page + 17., sections 8.9–8.10 and Example E8.4, printed p. 224 (PDF p. 241). Modern FCC performs most cracking in a dilute-phase riser; the regenerator uses a turbulent fluidised bed and the stripper uses steam contacting. The illustrated height 33 m and inside diameter 0.8 m come from the book's modelling example, not a universal size or a fabrication recommendation. Its catalyst and feed rates are 144 and 20 kg/s respectively, so C/O=7.2, different from Table 8.4 on slide 4. Geometry is set by capacity, gas expansion, velocity, residence time and solids circulation. The approximate plug-flow description is a modelling simplification. Rapid catalyst/vapour separation limits overcracking. The scale of vessel drawings is schematic.

### 9. Products and by-products

Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Printed page numbers are used. PDF page = printed page + 17., Table 8.2, p. 202. The tabulated ranges are indicative ranges across operating/feed cases, not simultaneous numbers that should sum to 100%. The book's combined dry gas + H2S category is retained as a yield category; usual refinery dry gas is primarily H2 and C1–C2, with composition affected by the separation cut. Gasoline is a blending component and may require sulfur reduction. LCO is aromatic and generally requires treatment/upgrading for diesel use. HCO/slurry are heavy aromatic streams with catalyst fines. Coke is deposited on catalyst and consumed in the regenerator; it is not recovered as a saleable FCC product. CO2/CO, SOx, NOx and catalyst fines are potential regeneration emissions requiring control, rather than part of the hydrocarbon yield basis.

### 10. Optimization and references

Lecture: Cracking and Reforming 1, supplied course PDF, pp. 15–17, 20, 24. Fahim, M. A., Al-Sahhaf, T. A., and Elkilani, A. S. (2010). Fundamentals of Petroleum Refining. Elsevier. Chapter 8, pp. 199–235. Printed page numbers are used. PDF page = printed page + 17., sections 8.5–8.7, 8.9, 8.12 and 8.16. This slide synthesises engineering trade-offs from the sources rather than claiming a universal optimum. Higher severity raises conversion but can consume gasoline to make gas and coke. ZSM-5 raises light olefin production and can sacrifice gasoline yield. Good feed atomisation and rapid separation improve selectivity. Catalyst/oil ratio, regenerated catalyst temperature and feed preheat are coupled by the heat balance. Optimization must respect air blower, wet gas compressor, regenerator temperature, catalyst circulation and emission limits. Check actual plant yields and product quality before choosing setpoints.
